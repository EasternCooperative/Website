excerpt: 'Partners take turns exploring a quiet trail with eyes closed while the sighted partner guides them and introduces things to touch.'
origin: 'Contributed by Nan Fawcett Cope to Of Play and Playfulness (ECRS, 1990).'
instructions: |
  Choose a clear and level trail in a natural setting such as a woods or meadow or along a stream. Allow each member of the group to choose a partner, and be available to join someone who does not have a partner.

  Explain that each partner will have the chance to explore the surroundings without the benefit of sight for several minutes, while the sighted partner acts as a guide. Have blindfolds available for people who want them, but just closing the eyes interferes less with other senses. Make sure that the importance of the guide role is clear: the guide keeps in close physical contact with his or her partner, makes sure the way is clear, helps to introduce objects, and gives a sense of safety to the temporarily blind person. Encourage the participants to keep noise to a minimum, and make use of their senses other than sight.

  Then start couples, one at a time, slowly down the trail, allowing enough space between each couple so that they are relatively unaffected by those in front or in back. Allow 5-10 minutes, and then let the sighted and unsighted partners change roles.
leadingTips: 'It is good to have time available at the end for people to share their discoveries or reactions. The experience can be a powerful one, and people want to talk about it.'
