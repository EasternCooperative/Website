excerpt: 'A Japanese team game: one player from each team chooses Old Woman, Warrior, or Lion behind a screen, and they meet face to face on a signal.'
origin: 'Japan. Written up in Of Play and Playfulness (ECRS, 1990).'
objective: 'For a team to win by scoring over the last player of the opposing team. Warrior (samurai) overcomes the Lion (shishi); Lion overcomes the Old Woman (obaasan); Old Woman, by her wisdom, is superior to the Warrior.'
instructions: |
  **Formation:** 10-18 players, divided into two teams. All are seated facing the front of the room. A screen is placed about 10 feet in front of the players, perpendicular to them. During the game, this screen will hide two players from each other, while leaving them visible to the other players.

  One player from each team goes to that team's side of the screen, and is hidden from the opponent but visible to the seated teams. Each chooses one of the three roles to portray, and assumes the appropriate position:

  - **Obaasan (Old Woman):** bent over, leaning on a cane.
  - **Samurai (Warrior):** stands straight with a hand on the hilt of an imaginary sword.
  - **Shishi (Lion):** on all fours.

  On a given signal, the players advance along the screen and confront each other as they emerge in front of it.

  The player who wins the round resumes his or her place behind the screen for the next round; the one who lost returns to the team and a new player takes the vacant space at the screen.

  The game continues until all the players of one team have had their turn, while the winning team has only used some of its members.
language: 'Japanese'
translation: 'Old Woman, Warrior, and Lion.'
leadingTips: |
  In North America, the idea that not all members of the winning team will have a turn may be seen as "unfair." The leader may decide to play the game until everyone has had at least one turn.

  *Obaasan* expresses the Japanese belief in the superiority of the wisdom of the aged, particularly women, over the prowess of the warrior class, even as it acknowledges the physical strength of the lion. It keeps the team allegiance of *El Hombre* while asking each player to decide alone, so don't substitute team decision-making: that loses the individual responsibility at its heart (Hal Kantor).
